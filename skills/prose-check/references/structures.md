# Structural review signals

Patterns become AI tells through repetition, predictability, and lack of
context. Do not treat a grammatical structure as evidence by itself. Review
the passage, the brand, and nearby copy before changing it.

## Contrasts and reframes

Patterns such as "not X, but Y" can feel formulaic when every section uses one
to manufacture a reveal. They can also express a real distinction clearly.

Keep the contrast when both sides matter. Revise it when X is disposable
runway and Y carries the whole point.

## Fragments and short sentences

Fragments can create warmth, humor, urgency, or an intentional spoken rhythm.
They become distracting when several are stacked for artificial drama.

Do not combine fragments merely to make them grammatically complete. Read the
passage aloud and preserve intentional cadence.

## Rhetorical questions

Questions can invite reflection, anticipate a reader's concern, or support a
playful brand voice. They feel mechanical when the writer asks and immediately
answers the same kind of question throughout a page.

Keep useful questions. Remove setups that only announce the next sentence.

## Agency and passive voice

Prefer a named actor when responsibility matters. Passive voice is appropriate
when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, obvious, or intentionally de-emphasized.

Examples:

- "We deleted the file" is clearer when the product performed the action.
- "Your file was deleted" may be better when the result matters more than the
  internal actor.
- "Mistakes were made" is evasive when someone must take responsibility.

Do not force "we" or "you" into every sentence. False intimacy, invented
agency, and accidental blame are worse than a natural passive construction.

## Inanimate subjects

Normal English gives agency to products, documents, markets, data, and ideas:
"the report shows," "the market reacted," and "the guide explains" can be
clear and human. Revise only when the construction hides a responsible person,
creates ambiguity, or conflicts with the brand.

## Sentence openings

Sentences may naturally begin with What, When, Where, Which, Who, Why, How,
"So," or "But." Review repeated openings across a passage, not isolated uses.
Do not contort a clear sentence to satisfy a word-position rule.

## Lists and repetition

Two-item and three-item lists are both valid. Repetition can create emphasis
and cohesion. Change them when the rhythm becomes predictable or the extra
item adds no value, not because of a numeric rule.

## Em dashes

Em dashes are the one universal mechanical check in this skill. Replace each
one while preserving pace and emphasis. Choose a comma, colon, parentheses, or
new sentence based on how the original reads. Do not insert a spaced hyphen as
a substitute.

## Extremes and absolutes

Words such as "always," "never," "everyone," and "nobody" can overstate a
claim. Check accuracy. Keep them in idiom, humor, quoted speech, or a statement
that is actually absolute.
